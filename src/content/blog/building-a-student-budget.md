Most student budgets do not fail because the arithmetic is wrong. They fail because they were written for an ideal month: rent, a modest food shop, and a vague promise to "spend less". Then a textbook, a birthday, a train ticket home and a flat tyre arrive in the same fortnight, and the plan stops describing reality.

A budget is not a prediction. It is a plan you adjust, and the useful version is the one you can keep in a normal, slightly chaotic month.

## Start from the money that has actually arrived

Write down the money you have and where it comes from, with dates:

- **Maintenance loan or grant instalments.** Note the date each one lands.
- **Wages.** Use the hours you are actually scheduled for, not the hours you hope to pick up.
- **Scholarships, bursaries or departmental payments.** Check whether they are paid once or in instalments.
- **Support from family.** If it is regular, treat it as income. If it is occasional, do not build it into the plan.

The important detail is timing. A termly loan is not monthly income. If you spend the October instalment as though a third of it belongs to each month, November and December get very thin. A simple fix is to decide, on the day the money arrives, how much of it belongs to each remaining month of term and set the rest aside in a separate pot.

## Write down the commitments that are already decided

These are the costs you have already agreed to, so they come first:

- Rent, or your contribution to it, and any utilities you pay directly
- Phone contract and any insurance you are liable for
- Travel to campus: a season ticket, a bus pass, or fuel and parking
- Course costs you know about: printing, lab equipment, field trips, software
- Memberships and subscriptions you intend to keep
- Anything you are paying off in instalments

Use the real amounts from your bank statement rather than memory. A £9.99 subscription remembered as "about a fiver" is exactly the kind of error a budget is meant to catch.

It also helps to be honest about which items are genuinely fixed and which are only fixed by habit. Rent is fixed. A gym membership is a decision you can revisit.

## Estimate the flexible part from a normal week

The flexible part is food, travel, social spending, laundry, and the small purchases that never feel like spending at the time. Estimating this for a whole month in one go is hard, so start with one ordinary week and multiply:

| Item | Typical week | Month (× 4.3) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food shop and campus lunches | £35 | £150 |
| Local travel | £12 | £52 |
| Social, including one evening out | £20 | £86 |
| Laundry and household bits | £5 | £22 |
| **Total** | **£72** | **£310** |

These numbers are illustrative, not a benchmark; your own week will look different, and that is the point. Multiply by 4.3 rather than 4, because a month is slightly longer than four weeks and the error compounds over a term.

If you have no idea what your week costs, use your last thirty days of transactions as the estimate. It does not need to be perfect. It needs to be closer to reality than a guess.

## Give irregular costs their own monthly line

Rent deposits, textbooks, annual insurance, travel home and repair bills do not arrive monthly, but they are still part of the year. Divide each one by the number of months until it is due and add that amount to the budget as a set-aside. We cover the mechanics in [planning for irregular costs](/blog/irregular-costs/), but the principle belongs in the budget itself: if it is not a line, it will become an emergency.

## Add it up and decide what has to change

Now do the subtraction:

> income for the month − commitments − flexible living costs − irregular set-aside = what is left

If the answer is positive, give it a job immediately: a savings buffer, a deposit fund, or a small unallocated margin for the weeks when the plan is wrong. Money without a job tends to disappear politely.

If the answer is negative, do not start by cutting food. Work through the list in this order:

1. **Anything you pay for but do not use.** Unused subscriptions and memberships are the cheapest cuts available.
2. **Frequency, not category.** One takeaway a week instead of three changes the total; banning takeaways entirely usually does not last.
3. **Convenience you are paying a premium for.** Delivery fees, short-notice bookings, buying lunch every day.
4. **Social and travel.** Adjust these carefully; they are usually the reason the week is worth having.
5. **Food and course materials last.** Cutting these makes everything else harder and rarely saves much.

"Aim to spend less" is not a plan. "Two fewer deliveries a month and pause the second streaming service" is.

## Budget the month, check the term

The month is the right unit for decisions because that is how most bills and habits work. The term is the right unit for sanity checks, because that is how student income works. Once a term, compare what you expected to spend with what you actually spent, and adjust the next term's numbers rather than this month's.

## Make it survive a bad week

Three habits keep a budget alive:

- **One place for the numbers.** If transactions end up spread across an app, a notes file and a shoebox of receipts, none of them stays up to date. UniBudget exists to keep this simple: transactions in one list, one budget for the current month, and a running total of what is left.
- **A short weekly check.** Five minutes is enough to notice that you are two days from the end of the month with four days of budget left. There is a [weekly routine here](/blog/weekly-money-check-in/).
- **One adjustment at a time.** When you overspend, reduce a category for the rest of the month. Do not throw out the plan and start a new system on the first of next month.

A budget you keep is worth more than a perfect one you abandon in week two.
