A monthly budget handles the ordinary weeks well and then gets ambushed. The ambush is not bad luck: the same costs appear at roughly the same points every year. A deposit before the next tenancy starts, textbooks in the first fortnight of term, a train home at the end of term, insurance renewing in the same month as everything else.

The fix is not a bigger budget. It is giving each of those costs a date and a monthly line of its own.

## Make a list with dates

Write down every cost you know is coming in the next twelve months, even if the amount is approximate:

| Cost | When | Rough amount | Months to save |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deposit for next year's flat | September | £450 | 9 |
| Textbooks and course kit | Start of term | £120 | 2 |
| Train home at the end of term | December | £180 | 6 |
| Laptop repair fund | Unknown | £250 | 12 |
| Annual insurance | March | £90 | 5 |
| Course field trip | April | £160 | 6 |

Two things happen when you write this down. First, the costs stop feeling like emergencies; they are scheduled. Second, the total tells you whether your monthly income can carry them at all, which is better to know in September than in December.

## Turn each one into a monthly set-aside

Divide each cost by the number of months until it is due:

- A £450 deposit in nine months is £50 a month.
- £120 of textbooks in two months is £60 a month.
- £180 of travel in six months is £30 a month.

Add those to your monthly budget as a line called something like "money already owed to future me". It is not spending, so it should sit separately from the amount you allow yourself for food and social life. Mixing the two is how a deposit fund quietly becomes a night out.

## Keep the pot separate from spending money

Money that is visible in your current account will be spent, not because you are careless but because it is there and the month is long. Move the set-aside to a different account, or at least to a separate savings pot with a name. "Deposit — do not touch" works better than an unnamed balance.

This is the same idea as a sinking fund: a small, boring, regular amount that turns a large future bill into a series of unremarkable ones.

## Deposits need a little admin

A deposit is the largest irregular cost most students face, and the easiest to lose money on through paperwork rather than bad luck:

- Get a receipt for the deposit and keep it with your tenancy agreement.
- Check what protection rules apply where you live; in many places a tenancy deposit must be held in an approved scheme, and the landlord or agent has to tell you which one.
- Photograph the property when you move in, including anything already damaged, and send the photos to the landlord or agent so there is a dated record.
- Ask in writing when and how the deposit will be returned, and what could be deducted.
- Expect a delay between moving out and getting the money back, and plan the next deposit with that gap in mind.

## Bills that arrive quarterly or yearly

Some costs are irregular only because of how they are billed. Energy, water, contents insurance and annual memberships often work this way. Find the billing frequency and divide it: a quarterly bill is a monthly cost that arrives three months late.

If you share a house, agree early who holds the account, how the bill is split, and what happens when someone moves out. A shared spreadsheet is less exciting than a group chat, and considerably more useful in February.

## If a cost arrives before you have saved for it

It happens, especially in a first year when you did not know the cost existed. Deal with it in this order:

1. **Protect the essentials.** Rent, food, travel to campus and course materials come before anything else.
2. **Talk to the provider before the deadline.** Instalments, an extension or a payment plan are usually easier to arrange before a payment is missed than after.
3. **Use the support that exists.** Universities typically have a money advice service, and students' unions often know which funds and hardship schemes are open. Asking early is not a failure.
4. **Do not quietly cover it with next month's rent.** That converts one late cost into a recurring problem.
5. **Restart the set-aside as soon as you can,** even at a smaller amount. The point is to keep the next occurrence from being an emergency too.

## Review the list once a term

Costs move. A course changes its reading list, a tenancy ends, a laptop gets older. Once a term, add anything new to the list, remove what has been paid, and recalculate the monthly set-aside. Ten minutes, three times a year.

A budget that only knows about this month will always be surprised. A budget that knows about next September stops treating September as bad luck.
