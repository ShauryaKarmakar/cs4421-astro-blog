A student budget rarely breaks because of one dramatic purchase. It leaks: a streaming service here, a delivery membership there, a phone contract that grew two add-ons, a gym you stopped visiting in November. Individually none of them feels like a decision. Together they can be the difference between a comfortable month and a tight one.

The good news is that leaks are unusually easy to fix once you can see them, because they repeat.

## The usual suspects

Start by looking for these patterns in your own statements:

- **Entertainment subscriptions.** Two or three services that each seemed cheap, often with overlap in what they show.
- **Music, cloud storage and app subscriptions.** Bought on a phone, billed forever, forgotten quickly.
- **Memberships.** Campus gym alongside a private gym, a society membership for a sport you no longer play, a club that auto-renews.
- **Delivery and shopping memberships.** These make each order feel cheaper while increasing how often you order.
- **Phone contract extras.** Insurance you did not need, a storage add-on, a plan step-up you never reversed.
- **Insurance add-ons.** Contents cover for a laptop that is already covered elsewhere, gadget policies with a limit worth less than the premiums.
- **Annual renewals.** Software licences, antivirus, storage, domain names, professional memberships.
- **Bank charges and overdraft fees.** Small, recurring, and easy to treat as normal.
- **Convenience fees.** Booking fees, delivery fees, ticket handling fees, and the "small order" charge that turns a £6 lunch into £9.

## Find them in one sitting

You do not need weeks of tracking to find recurring charges. You need one focused session:

1. Open the last sixty to ninety days of statements. That means every card, plus any wallet or payment app you use, plus anything a family member pays on your behalf.
2. List every charge that appears more than once. Write down the amount and roughly how often it recurs.
3. Mark the renewal date or billing day for each one.
4. Convert each to its annual cost by multiplying by 12. Nine pounds a month sounds modest; a hundred and eight pounds a year is a decision.

That single list is usually more useful than a month of guessing. It tells you both what you are committed to and when the next charge is coming.

## A fair test for keeping one

Not every recurring charge is a leak. Some are good value and genuinely used. Ask four questions about each:

- **Did I use it in the last thirty days?** If not, why is it still running?
- **Would I sign up again today at this price?** The honest answer separates habit from value.
- **Do I already have it?** Campus gym, library access, a family plan, a student discount, or a free tier may already cover the need.
- **Does cancelling break something I need?** Check the minimum term on a phone contract or insurance before you cancel.

If the answer is "I might use it later", pause it. Many services offer a pause or a downgrade; a paused subscription costs nothing while you decide.

## Cancel cleanly

Cancelling badly is how leaks reappear. A few habits make it stick:

- **Check the notice period.** Some contracts need thirty days' notice; cancelling the day before renewal may still charge you.
- **Cancel before the renewal date,** not after the statement arrives.
- **Keep the confirmation.** A screenshot or the confirmation email is your evidence if a charge reappears.
- **Delete the saved card** in shopping apps and browser checkouts. One-click purchasing is convenient until it becomes invisible spending.
- **Write the renewal dates down.** A short list of dates, checked once a month, catches the annual charges that a monthly review would miss.

## Watch what replaces them

Cutting three subscriptions frees money, but it does not automatically save money. Spending often drifts into whatever is easiest: more deliveries, more small purchases, a new service two months later. That is why the cut should be paired with a destination. Move the difference to an irregular-costs pot or a savings transfer on the day you cancel, and the leak becomes progress.

## Keep the review short

This does not need to be a monthly project. A ten-minute review once a month, plus a calendar reminder two days before each annual renewal, is enough. Sort your transactions so recurring charges are easy to spot: in UniBudget, entering each one as a normal expense under a consistent name makes the repeat visible when you look back over the month.

The goal is not to live without subscriptions. It is to make sure every repeating charge is one you would choose again.
