"I'll save whatever is left at the end of the month" is the most common savings plan, and the least reliable one. What is left at the end of the month is a result, not a decision. If saving depends on that result, it will happen in the calm months and disappear in the busy ones, which are usually the months it would have helped.

The alternative is smaller and duller: decide the amount when the money arrives, move it immediately, and let the habit do the work.

## Save on the day the money arrives

Pick an amount that you can name without flinching. It does not need to be impressive. Five pounds a week is £260 over a year, and a habit that survives a term is worth more than a larger plan that lasts a fortnight.

If your income arrives in one lump at the start of term, split it on arrival rather than at the end: a spending pot, an irregular-costs pot, and a savings pot. If you are paid monthly from a job, set the transfer for payday and, if your bank allows it, automate it. A transfer you do not have to remember is a transfer that happens in the weeks you are too busy to think about money.

## Build a buffer before you build goals

The first savings goal should be boring: a buffer that absorbs a surprise without borrowing. A useful way to size it is in weeks of essential spending, not in a round number.

Write down what one week of essentials costs you: rent contribution, food, travel to campus, phone, and any medication or care costs. That number is your weekly floor. Start by saving one week of it, then two. Many people never need more than a few weeks if their income is steady, and a buffer of a few weeks changes a broken laptop from a crisis into an inconvenience.

## Give later goals a name

Once there is a buffer, savings can have names: a deposit, a replacement laptop, a trip, graduation. Named pots are harder to spend because spending them requires deciding against the thing they are for. An unnamed balance is just money that happens to be elsewhere.

## Put the pot somewhere slightly inconvenient

The best savings account for a student is the one you do not see while buying coffee. A second current account with no card attached, or a savings account that is not displayed on your phone's home screen, adds just enough friction to stop an impulse transfer.

There is a trade-off: accounts with notice periods or fixed terms usually pay more but can lock your money away. If your buffer is the only money you have, keep at least that part accessible.

## When income is irregular

Part-time work, overtime, holiday jobs and bursaries rarely arrive evenly. Two approaches help:

- **Plan from the leanest month.** Build the budget around the income you can count on, and treat extra shifts as a bonus with a pre-decided destination: half to savings, half to the irregular-costs pot, for example.
- **Decide before the money arrives.** "When the summer job pays out, £300 goes to the deposit fund" is easier to follow than a decision made in the moment.

## If you are relying on an overdraft

If your account is usually overdrawn, saving can feel like a joke, and that is worth taking seriously rather than ignoring. Two things are true at once: an overdraft is expensive to live in, and reducing it is a form of saving too.

Look at the real position for one month: how much of the overdraft is structural (rent and bills arriving before income) and how much is spending? If it is structural, talk to your university's money advice service about timing and support options; if it is spending, the recurring-charge review may free up more than you expect. Overdraft terms and charges vary by bank and by country, so check your own agreement rather than relying on what someone in your seminar said.

## Rebuild without drama

Withdrawing from savings is allowed. A deposit, a repair, or a term with fewer shifts will sometimes use the buffer you built. The habit that matters is restarting the transfer on the next payday, at whatever amount is realistic that month, and treating a smaller restart as a restart rather than a failure.

## Keep it visible once a month

A savings habit survives on very little maintenance: check the balance once a month, confirm the transfer still runs, and adjust the amount when your income changes. That is the whole system. The point is not to optimise the interest; it is to make saving the default rather than the leftover.
